# Negotiation — Our Playbook (posture · landlord mindset · offers & documents)

*Our unified negotiation doctrine (consolidated July 8, 2026; sources in the ingestion ledger). ⚠️ The posture tactics, objection scripts, and financial-underwriting sections are INTERNAL — never client-facing, never content. The doctor-facing concepts (move-out math, options, "don't fixate on rate") are already routed to the content bank; the tactics stay here.*

> **Two mantras under everything:** (1) **Start with the end in mind** — the target comes from research, never from the other side's number. (2) **It's the client's offer, not ours** — every counter is agreed with the client before it moves.

## 1. Foundation — why any of this works
- **Nobody concedes without a fear of loss.** Landlords cut rate/add concessions only when they genuinely fear losing the tenant.
- **The value chain: face rate → NOI → cap rate → sale price.** The most recent lease benchmarks the whole property; one below-market deal can devalue a building by seven figures via cap-rate math — which is why landlords will hold space vacant for years rather than set a bad benchmark, and why they cling to face rate. **The killer math: +$1/SF on a 40,000 SF building ≈ +$40k NOI ≈ ~$570k more at sale (7 cap).**
- **Therefore the winning trade: concede face rate openly, extract everything else.** "You want $28 — you'll get $28, no argument. To get my client there I need six months' free rent, above-market TI, favorable commencement." The client cares about term + total cost; the landlord cares about rate + WALT. Give them rate and term; win the rest.

## 2. Posture — always hold real options (⚠️ INTERNAL craft)
- **Posture = the client holding legitimate, viable options. Always negotiate 3–4 properties simultaneously**, even when the client "found the one": landlords compete only when they know they're competing; deals die for random reasons; and the client makes a seven-figure decision with peace of mind. **If you don't hold options and a landlord calls the bluff, you failed the client** — like an attorney missing a filing.
- **Your compounding payoff:** the 3–4 landlords who missed this deal want the next one — and you hold their best-and-finals. Next client in the submarket skips months and starts beyond their last "best and final." This is the difference between 8 deals a year and 25.
- **Never bluff; every ask carries a factual reason.** "My other two options are at $60/SF TI; you're at $40 — get to 60 to stay competitive." Use client-protective constraints (lender rent cap) without exposing the client. And **sell the client's upside**: blue-chip healthcare tenant, <1% default use, invests $200k+ in the space, stays decades, brings traffic.
- **The six things the landlord must hear:** we're the exclusive agent (already hired) · we know the market · the client is educated · we're negotiating multiple options · *we'll* be submitting the offer, not receiving one ("send me an offer" is the worst thing a doctor can say to a landlord) · we help the client decide — no isolating the client from us.
- **Purchasing is the strongest posture** (nothing to benchmark against — make the deal or eat a vacancy). In a one-stoplight town: "if the building burned down, would you quit medicine or move a town over?" Everyone has options.
- **Tailor assertiveness to reality** — strong options: assertive; client won't actually move: soften ("we wanted you in the mix").

## 3. Think like the landlord (know who's across the table)
**By owner type:** *Mom-and-pop* — rate-flexible (vacancy hurts them personally), weak on TI (no $100k checks) → **make up TI gaps with free rent**; commission-sensitive; self-managed. *Institutional* — rate/NOI-driven (escalations ≥2.5% to show the street), free-rent-sensitive on renewals, but **TI is their strong card — ask well above market, they never lose a deal over TI dollars.**
**By asset:** *MOB landlords* actively want healthcare tenants (you're the outlet filling their building) — and medical office is only **~2% of the commercial market**, scarcity that cuts both ways. *Office/retail landlords* scrutinize the use (parking, mechanical, no synergy) and give less TI (a $170/SF medical build-out is worthless to the next office tenant) — always confirm the use is approvable. Retail increasingly wants healthcare on its pads.
**The golden rule of medical tenants: "they don't move" — good and bad news.** Landlords underwrite an **80–85% renewal probability**; your renewal job is to credibly disrupt that bet (the moment they learn the tenant wants to stay, concessions evaporate). On new deals, the same stickiness means landlords must EARN the relocation — put every concession on the table.
**What landlords actually optimize:** their equity investors' thresholds (IRR, equity multiple, exit cap), portfolio stability, tenant mix, WALT — usually NOT the rent itself. Most stuck deals unstick when you solve what the other side actually cares about (longer term, cleaner use, stability) instead of pushing harder. They underwrite the client like a bank — CARR sends client financials before LOI→lease; the "healthcare = 17-year-average, potentially 30–50-year tenancy (with a proper assignment clause)" case belongs IN the LOI.
**Landlord-broker reality:** they're under constant hire-and-fire pressure to show activity — showings and info should come easy; use it. And keep every listing-broker bridge intact — they supply the next deal too (coffee, market intel, easy commission conversations). Never adversarial: you'll sit across from them again on a space your client loves.

## 4. Offers & counters
**Purchases:** don't horse-trade toward the middle (split-the-difference is a trap). Anchor on research — comps, price/SF, condition, replacement cost, and the extra value of an existing healthcare build-out *to your client specifically*. Frame the client early for one of two worlds: **fairly priced** ("this is a great price" — then full price still feels like the win it is) or **overpriced** ("the data says $750k, not $1M — land near the data and we're great"). Know every negotiable item AND every player (bank, CPA, spouse) from the start — nothing worse than reopening a settled deal because an advisor surfaced late. Closing period: money goes hard after due diligence, so 10 days often beats the reflexive 30 — if the bank agrees.
**Leases (the most complicated transaction in CRE):** "everything's negotiable" means *many levers*, not infinite ones — you can't push hard on all of them. Pick by client: budget-maxed startup → **maximize TI**; second-gen space needing paint → chase **free rent, rate, parking** instead. Pull the TI lever and the rent lever moves — find the combination that's great for the client AND viable for the landlord (negotiate the landlord out the door and you failed the client, whose goal is a practice, not a trophy).
**Run every counter through the comparison sheets** (`../deal-analysis-toolkit.md`): they take the emotion out ("you love the awning? It costs $57,000 more — still love it?") and let you model structures ("$19 flat vs. $20/21 stepped = a $32k swing"). **Narrate the process to the client** — un-narrated wins read as "the landlord just gave it to us," and silence is the #1 cause of cold feet.

## 5. Documents — RFP vs. LOI
- **One sentence:** an **RFP** says *"prepare your best offer"* (rate line blank — landlord fills it); an **LOI** says *"we'd move forward on these terms"* (we dictate). Same document otherwise.
- **RFP is the default (70–90% of lease deals):** state everything that matters (build-out period → lease commencement; free-rent period → rent commencement — two separate rows; TI; escalations; exclusivity; commission) and ask for their most aggressive rate + escalation. **Send 3–4 RFPs** across the options — the mechanism behind negotiating multiple properties. Expect redlines back.
- **LOI carries commitment weight → only ONE live at a time** (two acceptances = a cornered client and a market reputation problem). Use an LOI on leases only when: one building, no leverage alternatives, or competing offers demand speed.
- **Purchases are LOI-only** (you dictate; no TI/free-rent concessions on a sale): price, deposit, DD period, contingencies (financing/appraisal/inspection/insurance — the deposit's backout protections), seller deliverables, commission. Don't run 3 purchase LOIs simultaneously.
- **⚠️ OUR-MARKET SPEC (critical for AL/FL):** the standard RFP/LOI HVAC spec (1 ton/350 SF) is for mild climates — **the Deep South needs ~1 ton per 250–275 SF** or the client eats a $10k+ HVAC surprise at signing. **Reset it on every AL/FL document.** Same review on electric: 3-phase/200-amp suits a ~2,000 SF dental office; large derm/vet needs more — confirm with the GC before it's a $5–15k gap.
- **Listing-agent handling:** "send me an LOI, not an RFP" → walk it verbally ("are you willing to do these things, and at what rate?") and hold the LOI until you'd commit to only their property. When they respond on THEIR form, go line-by-line — free rent and HVAC vanish. **The trap: "commission per separate written agreement" — never.** Commission is negotiated in writing from the start, or the end of the deal discovers "we only set aside 2%."
- **Moving-cost reality for relocations:** the disconnect/reconnect quote from the equipment supplier is the real number (~$15–60k); the movers are cheap. Factor it into the ask (a moving allowance is a legitimate lever).
- **Explain every ask — in a call AND an email** the listing broker can forward verbatim ("credentialing takes ~12 weeks, hence 3 months free rent"). Nine of ten brokers hand over an LOI with no explanation; being the one who explains wins deals.

## 6. Construction & concession mechanics (where clients quietly lose money)
- Landlord-managed construction carries a **5–6% PM fee — negotiate to 1–2%** when the tenant runs the job.
- **Free rent is cheap for landlords** (base-rent abatement sits outside the term and doesn't dent exit NOI) → push hard for it; **gross abatement (opex too) is the hard ask** — trade for extra base-rent months instead.
- **TI by space story:** shell space = aggressive TI (it's the landlord's value-add play); second-gen = "stabilizing a core asset," less TI. Converting shell: ask for an **NRC allowance on top of TI** (grid, fire/life-safety, HVAC can eat $35–40/SF of a "$50 allowance").
- **Amortized TI done right:** the amortization payment sits SEPARATE from base rent — never let escalations run on top of it. Interest is negotiable (6%, 8%, or straight-line 0%). Landlords aren't banks, but they're the recourse when the client's credit is maxed; ideal = one allowance up front and one amortized on top.
- **Allowances expire** ("use within 6 months") — move the client with urgency. **Negotiate WHEN dollars fund** (don't float the whole build). Watch landlords spending the TI budget on unapproved "upgrades" post-signing.
- **Off-hours/phased construction prices very differently** (a practice that can't close during works) — budget it.
- **Guarantees:** startups get personal-guarantee asks — negotiate **burn-off** (stepping down after year ~5 absent default). Letters of credit require real capitalization. **SBA snag: the bank takes first lien; a landlord demanding first position can stall a startup at the finish** — flag lien position early.

## 7. Reading the table (⚠️ INTERNAL)
- **Sometimes the landlord genuinely holds the posture** (a national tenant wants the floor; a drive-through pays double your client's use). Then don't push — lean on your other options. Know bluff (usual) from fact (occasional): a landlord with half a building vacant may still hold for the rate that cash-flows the asset.
- **Divide-and-conquer is their one move** — direct calls to the client, forwarded offers ("your agent wants $35k — I'd rather give it to you"), "I don't think they'll move." One successful call saves them six figures, so they'll try. The client responds to NONE of it; frame it as the insult it is ("they think you're weak and would rather gouge you").
- **"We have a policy" (no commissions / no free rent / no renewal concessions):** *"Who made the policy? You talked to yourself. My client's policy is that every landlord does these things — and your policy will cost you $400k when they move. If my policy cost me $400k and I was the sole decision-maker, I'd have a quick meeting with myself."*
- **"Would you pay a commission on a vacant space?" → "Yes" → "So a total stranger gets treated better than the tenant who's paid you for twenty years?"**
- **Conversion reality when posture is run properly:** legitimate requirements sign ~9/10; pipeline transacts ~8/10; renewals close favorably ~9/10. When payment genuinely can't be gotten, do it pro bono and convert it to testimonials + referrals.
- **In-person landlord meetings are data-extraction exercises** ("do you have real options or do you just love the space?") — the broker filters all options talk; face-to-face is for fire drills and architects, not negotiations.

# UX Doctrine — how every surface stays friendly

*SHARED-TIER design law, created 2026-07-13 (Joe's directive, "do all of this now"). Governs every surface a human touches (Joe or Dell) and every Claude reply that hands a human a choice: artifacts, the daily heartbeat, the Monday brief, digests, boards, and chat. Task-loaded via the root INDEX router on any user-facing build; the always-on parts (one action, feedback, plain language) are also pointed from 00_Context/ai-operating-notes.md and DNA/writing-rules.md. On a writing surface this sits ON TOP of writing-rules.md: writing-rules governs the prose, this governs the structure and the interaction.*

## The thesis (Tesler's Law)
Larry Tesler's Law of Conservation of Complexity: complexity cannot be deleted, only moved. It lives either in the machine or in the person. This system is intentionally complex under the hood; the whole job of the surface is to hold that complexity so Joe and Dell never feel it. Amazing and complex to build, simple to use. Every rule below is one way to push complexity off the person and into the system.

## The laws

1. **One home.** *(Dieter Rams, "less, but better"; John Maeda, Reduce; Hick's Law: choice time grows with the number of choices.)* There is ONE starting surface, The Front Door, and every other surface is reachable from it. Do not add a new top-level launcher; fold new entry points into the Front Door. Retire or tombstone redundant launchers so the user's mental map has a single door (Hot Leads is already folding into the lead board, open-loops #80).

2. **Vital few first (progressive disclosure).** *(Steve Krug, "Don't Make Me Think"; Nielsen, aesthetic and minimalist design; Maeda, Organize.)* Every surface leads with the handful actually used today; the long tail sits one tap deeper under "More" / "All actions". A brief shows what needs the person now, not everything the system tracks. Never show 48 of anything at once.

3. **One action per item.** *(Fitts's Law: big, close targets; Krug: self-evident; Nielsen: recognition over recall; and the standing rule "never make Joe remember the summons".)* Every item a human is shown ends in a single obvious next step, with the literal words to say or the tap to make (Send / Snooze / Skip), never an open "what do you want to do?" Show the status; do not make the person recall it.

4. **Say what changed, and make it reversible.** *(Nielsen heuristic 1, visibility of system status; heuristic 3, user control and freedom / undo; Don Norman, feedback.)* After every action, one plain sentence naming what changed and where. Every action is reversible on request: keep the prior state reachable (ledgers, Drive version history, the write-verify sandwich) and offer "say undo to reverse it". Nothing the machine does is opaque or one-way.

5. **Speak human, hide the plumbing.** *(Don Norman, match between the system and the real world; Nielsen, consistency and standards; Rams, thorough to the last detail.)* On any surface a human reads, show names and plain language, never internal IDs (L-###, V-###, C-###, CMP-###) or engine jargon (ETL, "the wiring", "sweep"). Use one word per concept everywhere (Hot Lead, On Deck, Deal). IDs stay in the files as join keys; they never reach the eye. If an ID must appear for support, it goes dim and secondary, never in the headline.


## Five more laws (added July 13, 2026, "build all of these")

6. **Smart defaults, aim for zero decisions not one.** *(Alan Cooper, "About Face"; Jared Spool; the default effect from Thaler & Sunstein's "Nudge".)* Wherever the system can infer the likely answer, PRE-PICK it and ask the person only to confirm or correct the exception. An intake interview proposes the probable segment, owner, next-touch date, and deal type; the person taps yes or fixes the rare miss. Defaults must be safe (never a default that fires anything external or destructive) and always visibly overridable. This extends law 3: the best item needs one tap, the better one needs none.

7. **Calm technology, inform without demanding attention.** *(Mark Weiser & Amber Case, "Calm Technology"; Golden Krishna, "The Best Interface Is No Interface".)* Notifications are quiet by default and escalate only when something genuinely needs the person now. Batch, do not ping: one considered daily nudge, not a stream, and every push earns its interruption. The ideal outcome is the one the person never had to act on because the system handled it and reported it in the next calm summary. Governs the daily heartbeat and every push.

8. **One card grammar, every item wears the same shape.** *(Adam Wathan & Steve Schoger, "Refactoring UI"; Brad Frost's atomic design; Rams, consistency.)* A lead, a vendor, a deal, and a brief line all render in ONE repeated shape so the eye learns the pattern once and reads everything faster. The canonical CARR item card, in this order: **[name in plain words]  ·  [one status chip]  ·  [one line of why it matters / the outcome]  ·  [one action with the literal words to say]**. Same order, same words for the same states, on every surface. Do not invent a new layout per board; reuse the grammar.

9. **Teach in the flow, not in a manual.** *(Don Norman, knowledge in the world not in the head; Kathy Sierra, "Badass: Making Users Awesome".)* Never hand the person a wall of capability to study. Offer the next useful ability at the exact moment it applies, "you just logged a vendor, want me to find clients for them?" One relevant next step beats a 48-item menu. This is how Dell gets fluent without reading anything, and how Joe reaches capability he has not tried yet (pairs with the expansion posture in ai-operating-notes.md).

10. **Frame everything as the outcome, not the mechanism.** *(Julie Zhuo on product writing; Clayton Christensen, Jobs-to-be-Done.)* Every tile, brief line, and confirmation names the WIN, not the plumbing: "a renewal that saves your client money," not "run the 5v10 fill-engine." People want the result; the machinery is ours to carry. This is law 5's twin: hide the plumbing AND speak the payoff. The ability catalog already leads with "what you get"; extend it to every surface.

11. **Production-grade craft is the floor, never at the cost of calm.** *(Design-tokens practice from Refactoring UI; WCAG 2.1 AA; Nielsen heuristic 5 error prevention and 9 recovery; prefers-reduced-motion.)* Every surface ships finished, accessible, and token-driven, not a sketch. Concretely: (a) **Tokens before layout.** The CARR system is CSS variables, never one-off values: palette (navy `#002F6C`, deep navy `#00224D`, orange `#F57F29`), type pair (Oswald display, Montserrat body), and the spacing, radius, shadow, and motion scales. New surfaces consume the tokens; they do not invent colors or fonts. (b) **Complete states.** Every interactive element carries default, hover, active, focus-visible, disabled, loading, and error, plus an authored empty state wherever a list can be empty. (c) **Accessibility by default.** Semantic HTML, full keyboard operation, a visible focus ring designed into the look (orange on navy, never removed), touch targets at least 44px, and `prefers-reduced-motion` honored with a clean static fallback; aim WCAG AA contrast. (d) **Failure is designed, never silent.** Offline, partial, or stale data shows an on-brand, plain-language state naming what happened and the next step (this is law 4 plus the system's own "stale beats silent"), never a default browser error or a blank. (e) **Authored copy only.** No lorem, no filler, no generic marketing voice; every label and empty line follows writing-rules and speaks the outcome (laws 5 and 10). (f) **No AI-trope tell.** Avoid the averaged look (system fonts + purple-on-white gradient + identical rounded cards + stock icons); the CARR identity already does.

   **What law 11 explicitly REJECTS** *(reviewed from an external "frontend design" prompt on Jul 14, 2026 and judged against laws 2, 7, and 8):* the operating surfaces are not marketing pages. We do NOT chase a "radical art direction," maximalism, or a fresh look per surface. "No two designs should converge" is the opposite of law 8 and of this system's whole value, which is that every surface feels like one calm family. We do NOT add decorative signature gimmicks, custom cursors, scroll-triggered reveals, or heavy texture that delay or crowd the vital few (laws 2 and 7). And the functional status glyphs (🔔 ♻️ 🔴 ⭐ 🕰 ⚡) STAY, because they are card grammar, not emoji decoration; restraint on emoji applies only to client-facing deliverables, where they read as informal. The craft floor above is the improvement worth taking from that prompt; the expressive-portfolio philosophy is not, for internal tools.

## How to use it
When you build or change ANY user-facing surface, run all eleven as a checklist before shipping. Both brains follow it (shared). It is a living doctrine: add a law only when it is a durable principle, not a one-off patch, and log the why in decision-history.


## Implementation note — deep links from artifacts (Jul 14, 2026)
Buttons/tiles that should open a Cowork session (Front Door, Lead Board, dashboards) cannot get "new chat + prompt auto-filled" from inside an artifact — the app either pastes into the current chat or opens a blank new one. Standard pattern for law 3's "one action with the literal words": `target="_blank"` (opens a fresh chat) PLUS copy-the-prompt-on-click with a toast, so the user pastes. And never build tiles that open one artifact from another (persisted artifacts have no open-URL). Full detail: DNA/Team/front-door-build-note.md.

## 01 · Carousel: The lease has terms beyond rent

Healthcare real estate is full of numbers that hide in plain sight.

The base rate gets all the attention. Escalations, operating expenses, signage, options, build-out timing, and assignment language can change the deal just as much.

A space can feel right on the tour and still be wrong on paper.

Before you sign a medical office lease, make sure somebody is reading the whole deal.

## 02 · Carousel: One purchase. Two deals.

Buying a practice usually means making two decisions at once: whether the practice is worth buying, and whether the real estate attached to it will hold up.

The practice can be solid and the lease can still box you in for the next decade. The building can be a great asset and the business can still be the wrong fit.

Both deserve due diligence before either closing date.

## 03 · Carousel: Renewal leverage starts at 12 months

A renewal letter arriving 30 days before expiration leaves a practice with a deadline and very little room to negotiate.

Start around 12 months out. That gives you time to compare locations, create alternatives, and decide whether renewing is your best move.

Time gives a practice room to create leverage.

## 05 · Carousel: A larger TI package can still carry a bad rent

Tenant-improvement dollars matter. Rent keeps adding up for every year of the lease.

A landlord may be more comfortable contributing upfront dollars than lowering a rate that affects the value of the building.

Negotiate the whole economic picture. The biggest concession in the room may not be the one that matters most.

There is no single “Panhandle rate.” Pensacola, Fort Walton Beach, Panama City, Destin, and Tallahassee may share a map, but they do not share the same supply, traffic patterns, referral flows, or landlord leverage.

A practice looking at a move needs a search area shaped by patients and operations.

## 07 · Reel: What a site tour can’t show you

On a site tour, everybody looks up first. They notice visibility, parking, signage, and the space itself.

The quiet risks live in the documents. Is your use protected? Can the landlord recapture the space? Does the assignment language survive a practice sale? Who controls the build-out timeline?

The tour tells you whether a space feels right. The lease tells you whether you can stay there.

Your attorney should review the legal language. We make sure the business questions are on the table before it gets that far.

## 08 · Carousel: The cost of waiting shows up before the deadline

A practice owner can wait too long to address a renewal and still renew on time. That does not mean the delay was free.

When alternatives are gone, every ask gets harder: rate, free rent, build-out dollars, options, even the ability to walk.

The best time to build leverage is before you need it.

## 09 · Single image / carousel: The lease has to work on the day you sell

When you sell a practice, the real-estate agreement follows the practice. A buyer, lender, attorney, and landlord can all have a view of that lease.

The assignment language, renewal term, rent, and options can affect what the next owner is willing to take on.

The lease is part of the exit plan long before you are thinking about exiting.
